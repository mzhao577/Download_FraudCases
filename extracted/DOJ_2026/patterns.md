# Pattern analysis

## Recurring scheme patterns

### 1. Order-generation pipelines: telemarketing → paid signature → supplier billing
This is the single most reproduced architecture in the corpus, and it accounts for most of the very large dollar figures. The mechanics are consistent to the point of being a template:

1. A call center (often offshore) harvests beneficiary identifiers — Egypt-based centers for Michael Kochen/Sandro Herek; the Philippines for Robert "Bobby" Leon Smith III; Utah and Russia for the Brian Sutton organization; unspecified overseas centers for Joel Rufus French and Dr. Scott Taggart Roethle.
2. A "telemedicine" entity buys a physician's signature on pre-populated orders at a flat per-order price — $20 per prescription for Dr. David Becerril (who spent ~26 seconds per review, signing for deceased patients and amputees), ~$30 per order for Roethle ($674,000 across five companies) and Dr. Robert Tassin. Tommie Robinson, Simon Grinshteyn, Dr. Alexandria Williams, Timothy Sutton, Scharmaine Lawson Baker and Dr. Shayasta Mufti all appear in the same signer role.
3. The signed orders are sold, sometimes repeatedly, to DME suppliers and labs (Christopher Harwood/TelevisitMD, Teflyon Cameron, Reinaldo Wilson), or software intermediates the whole flow — Brett Blackman's DMERx/HealthSplash platform generated the orders that supported over $1 billion in billing.
4. Suppliers bill Medicare, and increasingly Medicare Advantage, TRICARE and CHAMPVA (French, Mark Loftis, James Thomas Foley).

Products rotate but the pipeline does not: orthotic braces, cancer/cardiovascular genetic tests (Keith J. Gray, $328M; Sean Alterman, $52M; Emylee Thai/Jamie McNamara), continuous glucose monitors, urinary catheters, compounded "foot bath" and pain creams (Frank Suess, Main Avenue Pharmacy), and most recently amniotic wound allografts and skin substitutes (Brian Rowan, ~$1.2B; Marizel Yukee, ~$906M; Leigh Tesar, $118M; Expert Wound Care, $46.6M). "Doctor chasing" faxes falsely asserting a pre-existing patient relationship appear in Alterman and Foley.

### 2. Straight phantom billing, usually detectable by a calendar
Claims for services that demonstrably could not have occurred recur across every provider type: Yolanda Matthews billed psychotherapy for hospitalized and deceased beneficiaries; Marisol Rodriguez billed 15,000+ medication-management claims for patients who were hospitalized, incarcerated, deceased, or had left her practice years earlier; Violetta Mailyan billed Botox on days her clinic was closed, while she was abroad, and for an incarcerated beneficiary; Lorrie Laurel (Absolute Physical & Aquatic Therapy) billed 503 therapy claims for dates she was on cruises; Natasha Allmon billed more than 24 hours of counseling in a single day for her own family members; Steven A. Price billed 30+ crown lengthenings on one patient. Pharmacies show the inventory version of the same tell: four Hudson County pharmacies, Trang Pharmacy, Rabih and Mohammad Hamdan, and Ali Naserdean all billed drugs they never purchased, and Dr. Claribel Tan billed 4,829 units of injectable biologics while purchasing 369.

### 3. Kickbacks laundered through professional-services paperwork
Where a payment must be disguised, the disguises are few and repeated:
- **Per-test payments dressed as rent or administrative fees** — the transcranial doppler cluster (David Fuhrmann, Timothy Doyle, James Rausch, ~$70.6M billed; Mitchell Eye Center; five Florida ophthalmology practices; Fromer/Floral Park; Scranton Heart Institute) and Modern Nuclear's above-FMV PET "supervision" fees.
- **Sham consulting agreements** — SpineFrontier paid surgeons $250–$1,000/hour for feedback they never gave; E. Bruce DiDonato's monthly "consulting fees" were computed as a percentage of the prior year's Medicare reimbursements on referrals; Boston Heart routed payments through MSO "investment distributions."
- **Marketing/lead contracts with per-referral economics** — Jonathan Gholston ($200/referral, payable only if the claim paid), Patrick Cassells, Deane Gilmore, Allin IP DX, Advanced Pathology Solutions' 4% volume commissions.
- **In-kind arrangements** — free lab set-up inside referring practices (Advanced Pathology Solutions' "lean labs"; NeoGenomics' below-FMV consulting), free samples and denied-claim reimbursement (EyePoint's DEXYCU "Assurance Program"), meals/golf (ASD/Oncology Supply), and speaker programs (Takeda; Veloxis, which also falsified expense reports to defeat Open Payments reporting).

Beneficiary-side inducements are cruder and equally common: gift cards tied to attendance days (Life Touch/Francine Sims Super, >$1M), hotel rooms (Divine Youth, >$470,000), cash and controlled substances to induce ride requests (Carl's Cab, Latham Taxi), cash and supermarket gift certificates to fill prescriptions and attend adult day care (Taesung Kim, Inwoo Kim), and "Monkey Bucks" scrip at the Celina, TN pharmacies.

### 4. Insider bribes for patient data
Distinct from marketer kickbacks: Ruby Scott paid a Detroit hospital discharge nurse over $130,000 (~$300 per billable patient, via CashApp/PayPal) for faxed Medicare patient records; Kenia Marrero bought lists of ~100 beneficiaries at ~$500 from a hospital-network employee and resold them for up to $7,000; Heriberto Rivera paid a VA Community Care scheduler to steer veterans to his clinic; ten Amtrak employees sold access to their own plan coverage.

### 5. Ownership concealment and re-enrollment after enforcement
Exclusion, suspension and criminal history are routinely engineered around. Armen Muradyan paid a friend $2,000/month to hold his lab's Medicare enrollment and bank accounts; Nita Palma used a nominal CEO across three hospices; Ronnie Robinson and Camille Childress concealed prior exclusions/convictions on enrollment; Charles Griffin re-entered SC Medicaid through his wife's company; Christian Cruz hid a felon co-owner; the Savani brothers rebilled Pennsylvania Medicaid through nominee-owned dental practices after contract termination; Kartik Bhatia and Jeanett Valenzuela Ayub simply opened new DME entities after CMS payment suspensions; the Ohio behavioral-health defendants kept billing through a second entity after losing credentialing.

### 6. Credentialing and impersonation fraud
Two variants. Individuals fabricating licensure to obtain employment and generate billable encounters: Heather May Wilbanks Greene (30+ facilities on two stolen RN license numbers), Niki Rashel Cook, Leticia Gallarzo, Christine Reese, Angela Boston, Lonnie Releford, Carlos Rendón Bermúdez. And organizations substituting cheaper labor while billing at licensed rates: Hertel & Brown (unlicensed technicians billed as PTs), Arthritis & Rheumatology Center (unlicensed MAs giving chemotherapy infusions), Addiction Recovery Care and Recovery Center of Kentucky (lower-level staff billed as higher-licensed clinicians), Felipe Ruiz (a drug sales rep performing debridements), Tri-Area Community Health (pharmacist-run wellness visits billed under physician names), Circle Medical (rendering NPI ≠ actual clinician), and Blanca Cardenas, whose NPI was used while she was in federal prison.

### 7. Time- and unit-based inflation in home and community services
Personal care, adult day care, waiver and behavioral-health programs are billed on time units that nobody independently observes. Tony King billed 10 hours where ~5 were provided; Chenelle Wright's day treatment used pre-filled notes showing 2:00–7:00pm when children arrived at 4:30; Serenity Keepers billed a flat six hours/day of peer support regardless of delivery; 1 Family Clinic averaged >12 hours per member per day at a clinic open eight; the Eastern District of Pennsylvania aides billed while incarcerated, overseas, deceased, or driving for rideshare. In Minnesota's Housing Stabilization Services program, defendants recruited beneficiaries at shelters and, when payers requested documentation, generated it with ChatGPT (Brilliant Minds Services; Jefferson/Brown).

### 8. Code manipulation by otherwise legitimate providers
Group therapy billed as individual (Team Rehab, Segal Arts, Hertel & Brown, ARC); modifier 25 appended to injection visits (Ophthalmic Consultants of Boston); panels unbundled after a panel code was denied (FidaLab); presumptive plus top-tier definitive toxicology billed simultaneously (Labcorp's ToxAssure); reflex "special stains" ordered before pathologist review (Advanced Pathology Solutions, Atlanta Gastroenterology); RUG-level inflation in SNFs (Symphony facilities, Stern Therapy/RegalCare); repeated multiplexed urine screens billed as nine separate collections (Don J. Wagoner).

### 9. Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment inflation
A structurally different scheme, run by large organizations: one-directional chart review that adds codes but never deletes unsupported ones (Aetna), retrospective "addenda" months after visits with physician bonuses tied to diagnosis-adding targets (Kaiser, $556M), assessment vendors reporting chronic conditions unsupported by testing and never reported by treating providers (Matrix/HealthFair), and an MSO sharing capitation upside while pushing HCC 55/59 codes (Complete Health).

### 10. Money-laundering infrastructure as a separate service layer
Several cases involve no clinical actor at all: Renat Abramov opened nominee accounts for a Russia-based organization; Elnar Zarbailov and Nikolai Buzolin moved DME proceeds offshore; Bendeliani/Danelia converted ~$13M to cashier's checks and wired it abroad; Irakli Nakashidze wired ABRH proceeds to China and Hong Kong; Habroon Habib registered a Connecticut shell whose claims were submitted from Pakistan. The "bust-out" pattern — enroll, bill enormous volume in weeks or months, extract cash, disappear — is explicit in ABRH ($179M in six months), Verisola ($400M), Oregon Clinical Laboratory ($46M in ~7 months), and the ~$3B catheter scheme.

## What the schemes have in common

- **The claim is usually true-looking and the underlying encounter is false.** Almost none of these are coding errors; they are manufactured documentation — forged signatures (Misty Dawn Woody, Brenda Lopez, HealthNow, Tactile Systems sales reps), backdated consent forms (Mailyan), fabricated progress notes (Monte Vista, ODA Solutions, Mustafa Hares's Mexico-based note writers), and AI-generated beneficiary "consent" recordings (Amirali Bhimani; Steven Peyroux).
- **Separation of the person who bills from the person who signs.** The prescriber almost never sees the patient; the biller almost never touches the product. This division is what makes the schemes scalable and is also what makes them visible in referral-network data.
- **Exploitation of billing categories with weak verification**: items shipped to a home, tests run on a specimen, time spent in a house, and diagnoses that pay without a service. Where prior authorization was removed, volume exploded immediately — Paul Randall/Monte Vista Pharmacy billed $269M in under a year specifically because Medi-Cal suspended prior authorization.
- **Targeting of populations that cannot or will not complain**: hospice and dementia patients (Rowan, French), Native American AHCCCS members (Jimmy Muyumbu), homeless and shelter populations (AP of South Florida ACA scheme, Divine Youth, Minnesota HSS), children in after-school programs (Ohio, Lancaster/Transformation Services), and the deceased.
- **Money leaves fast and conspicuously**: Lamborghini Urus (Curry), Rolls Royce (Alterman), yacht (Cory Lloyd; ODA Solutions), gold bars and $126M seized (Gehrke/King). Structuring below $10,000 appears in Cruz.
- **Payers are hit in proportion to how loosely they verify, not how large they are.** Medicare dominates, but state Medicaid waiver and behavioral-health programs produce the highest per-case ratio of "impossible" service hours, and commercial/self-funded plans (Amtrak, ILWU-PMA, BCBS Michigan/Illinois, AFLAC) show the same mechanics at smaller scale.

## Detection signals for a predictive model

**Impossibility checks (highest precision, appear in dozens of cases):**
- Service date vs. beneficiary status: inpatient stay, incarceration, date of death, hospice election. Direct in Matthews, Rodriguez, Mailyan, Missouri personal-care defendants, Arshad, Federal Employees case, Colorado NEMT (~$165,000 billed after death).
- Rendering provider unavailable: foreign travel, incarceration (Cardenas), employment elsewhere, unemployment claims (Rodriguez), clinic closure days.
- Aggregate service time per rendering NPI per day exceeding plausible limits (Allmon >24h; 1 Family Clinic >12h/member/day; Journey to Hope caseloads).

**Reconciliation checks:**
- Units billed vs. wholesaler acquisition data (Tan; Hudson County pharmacies; Trang; Hamdan pharmacies; Boerman's never-purchased wound products).
- Reported usual-and-customary vs. discount-program cash price (Ahold Delhaize).
- Claims vs. shipment/delivery evidence for DME.

**Network and concentration features:**
- Ordering-NPI concentration: one prescriber supporting orders across many unrelated suppliers/labs in distant states; extremely short or absent encounter records preceding the order; orders for anatomically impossible items (braces for amputated limbs — Becerril, French; ovarian/cervical cancer tests for male patients — Lawson Baker).
- Supplier–beneficiary geographic dispersion: 413 beneficiaries across 36 states for a single Connecticut shell (Habib); nationwide brace shipments from a single Florida address (Cruz).
- Marketer/lab pairs where payments track referral volume; shell-company payees with no clinical function.

**Enrollment and identity features (pre-claim):**
- New enrollee with immediate high-dollar volume in a single HCPCS family; billing velocity anomaly against enrollment age. ABRH, Verisola, Oregon Clinical Lab, Centennial Med Supply.
- Address/phone/bank/officer overlap with previously suspended, terminated or excluded entities (Bhatia, Valenzuela Ayub, Savani, Ohio behavioral health, Coastal Diagnostics sharing an address with a suspended lab).
- Owner disclosure inconsistencies and nominee indicators: owner with no industry history, multiple accounts across many banks, sudden control changes.

**Coding-pattern features:**
- Modifier 25 rate by provider relative to peers; ratio of one-on-one time codes to staffing capacity; presumptive/definitive toxicology pairing on the same specimen and DOS; reflex add-on stains without an intervening interpretation; panel components billed separately after a panel denial; per-patient repetition counts (42 stents in one patient — Serrano; 52 graft applications for one beneficiary — Expert Wound Care).
- Risk-adjustment-specific: diagnoses submitted only via chart review or addenda with no corresponding encounter claim or supporting clinical data (labs, BMI, imaging); one-directional add/delete asymmetry; HCC concentration among a single vendor's assessments.

**Peer benchmarking:** Rodriguez ranked first among 116 in-state APRNs — simple within-specialty, within-state volume ranking surfaced her.

**Beneficiary-behavior signals:** cost-sharing systematically waived or never pursued (Ellsworth, Kinex, Foley marketing); beneficiaries clustered around a single day-care/sober-home/shelter address; ride destinations to distant clinics inflating mileage (Arshad, Tri-Hamlet, Stevens's 400+ mile trips).

**Beneficiary-eligibility fraud** is a separate model: cross-state and cross-territory identity matching found ~495 identities drawing SNAP simultaneously in Massachusetts and Puerto Rico, which is exactly the join that supports the Massachusetts MassHealth cluster.

## Caveats and sample limits

- **This is a prosecution corpus, not a claims sample.** Every record is a DOJ or USAO announcement, selected for newsworthiness and dollar size. It contains no denominator, no non-fraud comparison population, and no false-positive information. Nothing here supports estimating base rates or scoring thresholds; it supports feature design only.
- **Amounts are not comparable across records.** The extracted FraudAmt field mixes billed, paid, and "intended loss," and includes roundup documents that aggregate hundreds of defendants ($14.6B Fraud Section year-in-review, $6.5B national takedown, $1.2B District of Arizona, $6.8B FCA annual report). Summing the amount field would double- and triple-count; the totals in the computed counts should be treated as unusable for magnitude claims.
- **The 463 documents are not 463 cases.** Several matters appear two or three times at different procedural stages (Merchia; Tan; Kumar; Popovych; Matrix/HealthFair; Cruz; Kochen/Herek; Abramov; the Savani brothers), and multi-defendant takedown releases from different districts describe overlapping conduct.
- **Not all of it is health care.** PPP/EIDL fraud, tax and payroll-tax cases, arson insurance fraud, coupon counterfeiting, card skimming, bid rigging, denaturalization actions and personnel announcements are in the set. Any model trained on the corpus label field would learn on contaminated data.
- **The fraud-type labels are loose.** "FakeEncounter" is applied both to services never delivered and to services delivered but medically unnecessary — clinically and analytically different problems requiring different detection features. "Falsify certificate" spans forged physician signatures, PPP eligibility attestations and fake nursing diplomas.
- **Several patterns rest on too few cases to generalize.** Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment appears in only a handful of matters (Aetna, Kaiser, Matrix/HealthFair, Complete Health), all large-organization civil settlements; that is enough to describe the mechanic but not to characterize prevalence or to claim it is growing. The same applies to the wound-allograft/skin-substitute cluster, which is prominent in 2026 filings but is concentrated in a small number of districts and defendants.
- **Geography reflects prosecuting district, not where care was billed.** Massachusetts's high count is largely the New England Strike Force prosecuting nationwide telemedicine/DME signers based elsewhere (Alabama, Illinois, Florida physicians). State counts should not be read as fraud density.
- **Sentencing figures are individual, not case-level.** The jail-months field frequently captures the longest sentence among multiple co-defendants, and many records are at the charged stage where allegations are unproven.
